
## 01:30 UTC — A name read as a behaviour, four times; rule and blindspot 46

The maintainer asked for an instruction file to stop me asserting behaviour I
had not checked. The honest answer is that the rule already existed —
`engineering-practices.md` § *Verify the artifact, not the report* — and that
it does not quite cover what I did, which is why a section was added rather
than a new file (the maintainer's own standing instruction: never duplicate).

The existing rule is about **software reporting success**. Today nothing
reported anything: I read a function's name (`refresh_game_patch`), a log
line's wording (`patch older than 86400s`), an issue's summary table ("both
entries are Bubble Bobble") and an empty-string comparison that printed
"SAME", and turned each into a claim about behaviour. Three of the four were
stated to the maintainer as fact before being corrected.

The fourth was caught by them, not by me: they reset two Bubble Bobble
achievements on the site, the device still read them as earned, and the rows
said why — `patch` 28 s old, `unlocks` 87,168 s old. `refresh_game_patch`
re-reads the set; `cache_unlocks` is a separate call the service's own pass
makes and my helper did not (`609c6a14be`).

The test that would have caught all four, now written down: **name the
artifact that would settle the claim, and read it.** "The row's cachedAt
moved" cannot be written without looking; "it refreshes the unlocks" needs
only the function's name.

## 02:10 UTC — The RAMD playbook's twelve principles, mapped against ours

The maintainer asked to adapt the twelve principles from the Awecelot RAMD
playbook into our instruction files. Checked rather than assumed: **ten of the
twelve already exist here under our own names**, spread across
`engineering-practices.md`, `decision-register.md`, `issue-tracking.md`,
`instruction-files.md`, `worktrees.md` and `vm-first.md`.

So the adaptation is an index, not a rewrite — `working-principles.md`, one
line per principle pointing at the rule that does the work. Their own
hub-and-spoke rule argues for exactly this: spokes reference, never duplicate.
Every pointer in it was verified against the actual headings before committing.

**Two were genuinely missing and are written out:**

- *Urgency increases rigour.* Nothing in the corpus said it, and today is the
  evidence: four claims made without checking, in one subsystem, while moving
  fast toward an RC. The artifact check is the last thing to drop, not the
  first; a deadline narrows scope, it does not lower the evidence standard.
- *Pre-flight reading.* `instruction-files.md` says which copy to read and that
  a stale worktree lies; it never said to read **before starting**. Now it
  does, with the registers included and a requirement to say which rules were
  read — because a rule consulted and a rule assumed produce identical output.

**One import is worth more than the rest: enforcement depth.** A rule goes
written → routed → evidenced → audited → self-examining. Most of ours sit at
written-and-routed, which is where decay happens (D-UI-039 lost 30 screens in a
month). What holds is audited: `pkgcheck`, `register-check`, `es-menu-map-check`,
`vm-qa`, `pre-push`. So the standing response to a rule broken twice is to move
it up a stage, not to rewrite it.

**One not adopted:** *creation over destruction*, a cultural stance for an
estate building products. Our equivalent instincts are already covered, and the
useful half — declaring what we deliberately do not do — exists as
`es-menu-map.md`'s `## Not mapped`.
